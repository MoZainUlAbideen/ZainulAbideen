Engineering notes · Earshot · October 2026 · 8 min read

The best retriever I built isn’t the one in production.

Earshot answers questions across podcasts and cites the exact second each claim was said. Here is how I chose its search design with an eval instead of intuition, why the most accurate option ships switched off, and how a plain string check made the citations trustworthy.

Try Earshot ↗ Source on GitHub ↗

The short version

  • I scored five search designs on 45 paraphrased questions over 875 passages, for recall, MRR and latency.
  • Plain hybrid search was worse than vectors alone at top-1 (0.51 vs 0.58). “Hybrid always wins” is a claim to measure.
  • A small cross-encoder reranker lifted recall@5 to 0.93, at 1.35 s per query instead of 69 ms.
  • The free host has 0.1 of a CPU, so production runs hybrid (0.76) behind a config flag. The reranker is one environment variable away.
  • A deterministic citation critic plus one repair pass took answered questions from 77.8% to 93.3%.

01 · The problemSearch where the answer is a moment, not a document.

Thousands of hours of expert conversation live in podcasts, and almost none of it is searchable. Finding “that bit where someone explained X” means scrubbing through hour-long episodes. Earshot transcribes episodes, indexes them, and answers a question across many episodes at once. The hard requirement is the citation: every claim must point to the second it was said, and clicking it plays the original audio from there.

That turns a vague goal (“good search”) into something measurable. A retrieved passage is only useful if it contains the answer, and a citation is only useful if it lands close enough to the true moment that a listener hears it. I used ±15 seconds as that bar.

  1. Ingest + queueRSS feeds deduplicated by fingerprint; jobs claimed with SKIP LOCKED in Postgres.
  2. TranscribeVoice-activity chunks, Whisper large-v3-turbo with word-level timestamps.
  3. IndexOverlapping 45 s passages, Postgres full-text + bge-small embeddings in pgvector.
  4. SearchKeyword + vector fused with RRF, then an optional cross-encoder reranks the top 20.
  5. Answer + verifyLLM answers in JSON with a verbatim quote per claim; plain code checks every quote.

The two highlighted stages are what this write-up is about. Everything runs in one Postgres database: queue, transcripts, full-text index and vectors.

02 · The evalFirst, a benchmark I could trust.

Comparing search designs needs questions with known answers. I had an LLM write 45 deliberately paraphrased questions for passages sampled from 9 episodes (875 passages in total), each with a verbatim evidence quote. Then I located that quote in the word timings to get the exact answer time for every question. Paraphrasing matters: a question that copies the transcript’s words flatters keyword search.

Two rules kept the eval honest. Recall is a lower bound, because only one passage counts as correct per question even when a neighbour also contains the answer. And I had already learned not to trust references blindly: in the transcription eval, a “hand-corrected” reference that started from a model’s draft kept 99.7% of that draft, so it measured agreement with the model, not accuracy. The scorer now flags any reference that changed less than 1% of its draft.

If the benchmark is wrong, every decision made with it is wrong too. So the benchmark got tests before the search did.

04 · The surpriseHybrid search lost to vectors alone.

The standard advice is that hybrid search (keyword plus vectors, fused with Reciprocal Rank Fusion) beats either half. On this benchmark it didn’t: hybrid’s recall@1 was 0.51, below vector-only at 0.58. Paraphrased questions share meaning with the answer, not words, so the keyword leg mostly adds noise that RRF then mixes into the top spots.

I kept hybrid anyway, for two reasons the averages hide. Exact-term queries (a file name like AGENTS.md, a person, a paper) are where keyword search earns its place, and the benchmark under-samples them. And hybrid’s recall@10 matches vector’s at 0.87, which is what matters when a reranker re-orders the top 20 next. The keyword leg also needed a fix first: websearch_to_tsquery ANDs every word, and natural-language questions rarely have all their words in one passage, so I switched it to OR semantics and covered that with a test.

“Hybrid always wins” is a claim to measure, not assume.

05 · Choosing a rerankerOne question behind, at half the latency.

Reranking was the big win: a cross-encoder reading the question and each of the top 20 passages together lifted recall@1 from 0.51 to around 0.80. I tested four. MiniLM-L-12 was the most accurate at top-1 (0.82), but jina-reranker-v1-tiny scored 0.80, one question behind on 45, at 1.35 s instead of 2.9 s. The turbo variant had the best recall@5 (0.96) but dropped to 0.67 at top-1, which matters because the answerer leans hardest on the first results. I picked jina-tiny as the default on the latency trade-off.

06 · ProductionWhy the best design ships switched off.

The live API runs on Render’s free tier, which gives the container 0.1 of a CPU. Reranking 20 passages there would take tens of seconds per question, which no one waits for. So production sets RERANK=0 and serves hybrid retrieval at recall@5 0.76, with a 69 ms search.

The important part is how that decision lives in the code: a config flag, not deleted code. The reranker path is still covered by tests and warms up at startup whenever it’s on, the /health endpoint reports which search mode is live, and the trade-off is written next to the flag with the measured numbers. When the app moves to a paid instance or a hosted reranker, recall@5 goes from 0.76 to 0.93 by changing one environment variable.

07 · CitationsA string check the model can’t talk its way past.

The eval exposed one more problem: passages are too coarse to cite. Even with reranking, the top passage’s start time landed within ±15 s of the true answer at most 40% of the time, because the answer sits around 15 seconds into a 45-second window. So answers don’t cite passages. The LLM must return JSON with a verbatim quote behind every claim, and a deterministic critic checks each quote word for word against the transcript and timestamps the citation at the exact word where the quote begins. Sentences without a verified quote are removed; if nothing survives, Earshot says it couldn’t find an answer rather than guess.

The first version was too strict. Classifying every refusal showed that 8 of 10 were the critic rejecting every quote, and none were retrieval misses. One repair pass fixed most of them: the model is told which quotes failed and why, and its new answer goes through the same check. The model also liked to quote the host’s question as evidence, so a quote containing a question mark is now rejected outright.

08 · Honest limitsWhat these numbers don’t prove.

  • A quote can exist without supporting the claim. The critic proves the words were said, not that they mean what the answer says. That gap is why citation accuracy is tracked separately from the answered rate.
  • 45 questions is a small benchmark. One question is 2.2 points of recall, so differences under about 0.05 are within noise. That’s part of why the reranker was chosen on latency, not on a one-question lead.
  • The questions were LLM-written. Paraphrasing on purpose makes them harder for keyword search, and real users may phrase things differently. Real query logs are the next evaluation set.
  • Latency was measured on a laptop CPU, not on the production host, which is exactly why the production host can’t run the reranker.

09 · NextWhat I’d do with more compute.

Turn the reranker on with a paid instance or a hosted model; add an eval gate in CI so a change that lowers recall can’t deploy; trace every answer in Langfuse with cost per audio-hour; and add speaker diarization so citations say who said it, not just when.
