A cited answer can still be wrong.

Rehnuma reads a photo of a Pakistani electricity bill, re-computes it rupee by rupee, explains it in Urdu and answers questions about NEPRA’s rules with the clause they come from. This is how it keeps a language model away from arithmetic, how Urdu retrieval went from 0% to 83%, and the one answer that was cited, confident and wrong.

The short version

  • The model never does arithmetic. A deterministic engine re-computes every rupee; the vision model only reads, and failed checks trigger a re-read.
  • The engine found a real Rs 2 inconsistency printed on a real bill, and a brute-force search showed no tax rule can produce it.
  • Urdu questions found the right NEPRA clause 0% of the time with keyword search. An LLM rewrite fused as a second query took Urdu top-5 retrieval to 83%.
  • The family’s own question got a cited, confident, wrong answer. The fix was structural, not a prompt tweak.
  • Across every summary model tested, zero invented numbers reached a user, because a critic rejects any number the engine didn’t produce.

01 · The problemA one-page bill almost nobody can read.

A Pakistani electricity bill stacks slab rates, protected-consumer status, fuel adjustments, quarterly adjustments, fixed charges, duty and GST onto one page, and most people ask the lineman what it means. Solar households also face NEPRA’s 2026 Prosumer Regulations, which changed what exported units are worth. The people who need help most read Urdu, not regulatory English.

That sets two hard constraints. Every rupee shown to a user must be right, because a wrong number on a bill is worse than no explanation. And every rule must come with the clause it’s from, because “the AI said so” isn’t something a household can take to their DISCO.

The design rule: the LLM never does arithmetic, and never has the last word on a fact.

  1. Read the photoGemini extracts the bill into a schema with no field for names, addresses or reference numbers.
  2. AuditA deterministic engine recomputes every line; failed checks send the photo back for a re-read.
  3. ExplainAn LLM writes the Urdu summary; a critic rejects any number the engine didn’t produce.
  4. RouteRules, not an LLM, decide if a question is about this bill, NEPRA’s rules, or both.
  5. CiteHybrid retrieval over 566 NEPRA clauses, an answer, and a critic that checks tags, numbers and language.

Highlighted: the two places where plain code checks the model’s work before a user sees it.

02 · The audit engineRe-computing a bill rupee by rupee.

The engine recomputes units from meter readings, the net-metering bank, electricity duty, GST, the fuel-adjustment tax cascade and arrears, using exact decimals. On three conventional IESCO bills it reproduces every tax line with zero delta, and each rule it encodes is tested against a rejected alternative. Electricity duty, for example, is 1.5% of the cost of electricity plus the quarterly adjustment, and including the fixed-charge surcharge would print 192 where the bill prints 169.

Then it found something real. On a March 2026 PESCO bill, the fuel-adjustment lines add up to Rs 1,166 but the printed total is Rs 1,164. Computing unrounded and rounding once reproduces the total exactly, which implies duty of about 14.57, not the 16 printed. A brute-force search over duty rates, GST bases and rounding orders found no rule that prints all three numbers together: the bill contradicts itself. The engine flags it, and a test requires every real bill to fail exactly its documented anomalies.

How it was found matters too. The first transcription read the duty as 15 and everything passed. The bill’s owner checked the paper, corrected it to 16, and the check failed. The label was corrected, not the tolerance.

On 2,000 synthetic bills with planted errors, the engine caught every one with zero false alarms.

03 · The vision loopA model that calculated instead of copying.

Gemini reads the photo; the engine checks the reading. Any failed check triggers a targeted re-read. On the first real run, 6 of 7 bills were read with 96.2% field accuracy and 100% on key fields.

Two bugs here were subtle. The re-read prompt first leaked the expected value (“expected 3273”), which would let the model copy the answer instead of reading it; it now names the failing fields only. And on a settlement month, the model calculated the net-metering bank (previous balance minus net units) instead of copying the printed 0. The engine caught it, but the re-read repeated the same calculation, so the prompt and the re-read feedback now say the bank is copied, never computed.

04 · Urdu retrievalFrom 0% to 83%, and why concatenation failed.

Rule questions search 566 clause-level chunks from five official NEPRA PDFs with lexical hybrid retrieval (BM25 plus character n-grams). The eval has 35 questions with a gold clause each: 22 dev questions seen while tuning, and 13 held-out questions written from the clause text before any tuning. 8 are in Urdu, the product’s default language.

With keyword search alone, Urdu questions found the right clause 0% of the time: the documents are in English. The fix was an LLM rewrite of the question into NEPRA’s wording. How it was added mattered more than the rewrite itself:

Shipped designThe setting that made English worseOther settings

Concatenating the question and its rewrite into one query diluted the user’s own precise words and dropped English hit@5 from 88% to 75%. Fusing them as two separate queries lets the rewrite add candidates without removing any. Then reading the rewrites showed the model appending document names (“NEPRA Consumer Service Manual”) to 6 of 27 English rewrites; those words occur all over the corpus and pulled answers toward billing clauses. They’re now stripped before searching.

One correction along the way: Reciprocal Rank Fusion produces exact ties, and 4 of 27 English questions tied at rank 1, broken by the order of chunks in the file. Some earlier top-1 numbers were partly that luck. Ties now break on BM25 rank of the user’s own words, a test shuffles the corpus and requires identical results, and every number here uses the new tie-break.

Rewrites were generated once and replayed for every setting after the first, so the rows differ only in retrieval, not in LLM sampling. Multilingual e5-small embeddings were tested too; they didn’t beat lexical retrieval in English, so they stay optional.

05 · The failure that matteredThe family’s question, answered wrong with a citation.

The held-out scores looked good. The important failure wasn’t in them. The question that started the project, asked in Urdu by the household whose bills these are: “my net metering was installed before 2026, at what rate are my units counted now?”

Rehnuma answered confidently, with a citation: the price stays under the old 2015 rules until the agreement ends, citing 2015 regulation 14(2). That’s wrong. The real rule is Prosumer Regulations 2026, 21(2): billing moves to net billing from the next cycle, with exports priced at the national average power purchase price until the agreement expires.

There were two causes. Retrieval returned five clauses of the repealed 2015 rules and missed 21(2). And each repealed source carried a note from my own metadata, a paraphrase of what it meant, which the model repeated as if it were NEPRA’s text. The fix was structural: the note no longer states any rule; every repealed document names its savings clause, which is always added next to any clause from it; and the critic rejects an answer that relies on a repealed source without citing that clause.

Then a second, quieter bug. The English answer became exactly right, but the Urdu one said the national average energy purchase price. In Urdu, the two legally different rates collapse into near-identical phrases, and the critic couldn’t see it because the numbers and citations were correct. Now rate names must appear verbatim in English inside any answer that discusses a price, checked against exactly what the model was shown.

The critic proved the answer was cited. Only reading it proved the answer was wrong.

Reading the drafts behind those failures found three more critic bugs: the sentence splitter broke on “Rs.” and “etc.”; a correct sentence was rejected for having its citation on the neighbouring sentence; and the retry prompt didn’t show the model its own draft, so a correct first draft was thrown away and rewritten wrong. Each one is fixed and tested.

06 · SummariesZero invented numbers, and a model that wrote “cat”.

The Urdu summary a household sees is written by an LLM and checked by a critic that allows only numbers the engine produced. If a draft fails three times, a template summary is used instead. Across every model tested on 7 real bills, no invented number reached a user.

The score table alone suggested two causes for allam’s failures, and both were wrong. Reading the drafts showed broken Urdu: repetition loops, “بلی” (cat) for “بل” (bill), and this month’s settlement reported as the account balance. It also showed that the critic can pass nonsense: one draft had every number right but swapped peak and off-peak. And both large models made the same false claim, “your credit is kept until 24 September”, which was my bug: the prompt passed the due date on credit bills. It no longer does.

07 · Honest limitsWhat these numbers don’t prove.

  • The critic checks numbers and citations, not meaning. A summary can pass with every number right and the meaning wrong. Human review by a native Urdu reader is the current safeguard, and so far that’s one reader.
  • The Urdu samples are tiny. 6 dev and 2 held-out questions: one question moves a row by 17 to 50 points. Read them as direction, not precision.
  • Questions, labels and router rules share an author. Real user questions are the real test.
  • A number can be checked against the wrong sub-clause. The critic asks whether a number appears anywhere in the cited clause; the eval’s “cites gold clause” column is what catches the rest.
  • The 12-month forecast has no accuracy number yet (n = 0) until same-household bill pairs exist, and a solar rupee forecast was dropped after “last year’s rupees” missed by 68%.

08 · EngineeringEvals as a build gate.

319 tests. Twenty eval metrics that need no API key are gated in CI against committed limits, so a metric that drops or disappears fails the build. A weekly workflow re-downloads NEPRA’s PDFs, compares SHA-256 hashes, and opens a review issue on any change; nothing updates itself. Langfuse traces every question from the API through routing, retrieval, each LLM call and the critic’s verdict, with long numbers masked and photos never sent. Bill photos are read in memory and never stored, and a test enforces that labels carry no identifiers.
